Exact Chebyshev-error reduction and its unresolved step
=====================================================

Definitions. For x>=1 let

  psi(x) = sum_{n<=x} Lambda(n),  E(x)=psi(x)-x,
  C = -(digamma(1/4)-log(pi))/2 = 2.6860917096...,
  J(x) = integral_1^x E(u) u^(-3/2) [1+(1/2)log(x/u)] du.

Here psi is Chebyshev's function; digamma is written out to avoid a
collision of names. All integrals up to a finite x are ordinary integrals.

1. Exact identity (unconditional)
--------------------------------

Write S(x)=sum_{n<=x} Lambda(n)n^(-1/2)log(x/n). Stieltjes integration
by parts gives

  S(x)=integral_1^x psi(u)u^(-3/2)[1+(1/2)log(x/u)]du.

The lower boundary is zero because psi(1)=0, and the upper boundary
is zero because log(x/x)=0. For the baseline psi(u)=u the integral is

  4 sqrt(x)-log(x)-4.

Thus formula (1.5) of Nakamura--Suzuki gives exactly

  g(log x)=J(x)+4-4/sqrt(x)+(C-1)log(x)
       +[Phi(x^(-2),2,1/4)/sqrt(x)-Phi(1,2,1/4)]/4.       (A)

No limiting interchange or assumption on zeta zeros enters (A).
The log coefficient is C-1, not C-2; replacing the Stieltjes integral
against d psi by an integral against psi without its lower-boundary
correction is one source of the latter error.

Define the positive constant and decreasing positive remainder

  K = sum_{k>=1} 1/[4(k+1/4)^2] = 0.2993322886...,
  R(x) = sum_{k>=1} x^(-2k-1/2)/[4(k+1/4)^2].

Then R(1)=K, R(x) decreases to 0, and (A) simplifies to

  g(log x)=J(x)+(C-1)log(x)-K+R(x).                       (B)

In particular, the x^(-1/2) terms cancel exactly between the explicit
archimedean exponential and the k=0 Lerch term. Remaining corrections
are O(x^(-5/2)); for x>1,

  0<R(x)<=x^(-5/2)/[4(5/4)^2(1-x^(-2))].

2. A useful weaker-looking sufficient inequality
------------------------------------------------

An unresolved sufficient target is simply

  There exists X>=1 such that J(x)<=0 for every x>=X.     (T)

This is weaker in form than the exact pointwise inequality
J(x)<=(1-C)log(x)+K-R(x) corresponding to g<=0. Target (T)
does not require getting the linear coefficient or constant sharp.
Nevertheless it remains RH-strength, for the following reason.

From (B), (T) gives g(t)<=(C-1)t eventually. Set
h(t)=(C-1)t-g(t) after a sufficiently large finite T, and h(t)=0
before T. Then h>=0. For Re(w)>1/2, its Laplace transform is the
sum of an entire finite-prefix correction and

  (C-1)/w^2 + (1/w^2)(xi'/xi)(1/2+w).

It is holomorphic near every positive real w, since xi has no zeros
on the real line there. If the abscissa of convergence were positive,
Landau's theorem for a nonnegative Laplace integrand would require a
singularity at that real abscissa, a contradiction. Therefore its
Laplace transform is holomorphic on Re(w)>0, excluding zeros of xi
in Re(s)>1/2. Functional symmetry then gives RH.

Conversely RH implies g is bounded, by its absolutely convergent
zero expansion, so (B) gives

  J(x)=-(C-1)log(x)+O(1),

which is eventually negative because C>1. Thus (T) is equivalent to
RH; it is not an unconditional estimate obtained here. More generally,
any eventual one-sided bound J(x)<=A(1+log(x))^m, with fixed finite
A,m>=0, suffices by using a polynomial majorant in the same argument.

An even looser growth formulation is

  for every epsilon>0, J(x)<=B_epsilon x^epsilon
  for all sufficiently large x.

For each epsilon, the epsilon-exponential majorant and the same
Landau argument exclude zeros with real part >1/2+epsilon. Letting
epsilon decrease to zero gives RH. All these formulations are
reductions of the same unsolved obstruction, not independent progress.

3. Why an infinite-moment cancellation is invalid
------------------------------------------------

For Re(s)>1 one has

  M(s)=integral_1^infinity E(u)u^(-s-1)du
      =-(zeta'/zeta)(s)/s - 1/(s-1).                    (C)

Since (xi'/xi)(1/2)=0, the analytic continuation satisfies
(zeta'/zeta)(1/2)=C. Consequently its value at 1/2 is

  M_cont(1/2)=2(1-C).

Formally this would cancel (C-1)log(x) in (B) when one expands
J(x)=(1+(1/2)log(x))I_0(x)-(1/2)I_1(x), where

  I_0(x)=integral_1^x E(u)u^(-3/2)du,
  I_1(x)=integral_1^x E(u)log(u)u^(-3/2)du.

But M_cont(1/2) is NOT the value of an ordinary improper integral.
In fact I_0(x) cannot converge, unconditionally, as follows.

Suppose F(v)=I_0(exp(v)) tends to a finite L. Integration by parts
would give, initially for Re(w)>1/2 and then throughout Re(w)>0,

  M(1/2+w)=w integral_0^infinity F(v)exp(-wv)dv.           (D)

The right side is holomorphic on Re(w)>0. Equation (C) would then
exclude zeros in Re(s)>1/2 and imply RH. Choose a nontrivial zero
rho=1/2+i gamma (gamma nonzero), of multiplicity m. Such zeros exist.
For w=epsilon+i gamma, convergence F(v)->L implies

  epsilon M(rho+epsilon)->0 as epsilon decreases to zero:

split F=L+(F-L), evaluate the constant part, and bound the tail
by sup_{v>=V}|F(v)-L| times epsilon integral_V^infinity exp(-epsilon v)dv.
The finite prefix vanishes after multiplication by epsilon. However,
(C) and the zero at rho give

  epsilon M(rho+epsilon)->-m/rho, which is nonzero.

This contradiction proves nonconvergence of the signed improper
moment. Absolute convergence is therefore impossible as well. A
meromorphically continued Mellin value cannot be substituted into
the finite-x identity (B) as a convergent moment.

4. What unconditional prime estimates give and fail to give
---------------------------------------------------------

For any hypothetical absolute envelope |E(u)|<=B u^theta, theta>1/2,
put a=theta-1/2. Direct integration bounds

  |J(x)|<=B x^a [1/a+1/(2a^2)]

apart from an immaterial finite initial interval. This is far larger
than the one-sided polylogarithmic or subpower target when a>0.
An absolute square-root estimate with logarithmic factors would yield
a polylogarithmic J bound, but such a square-root estimate is already
RH-strength.

The classical unconditional PNT envelope
E(u)=O(u exp(-c sqrt(log u))) yields by the same absolute-value method

  J(x)=O(sqrt(x) exp(-c' sqrt(log x)))

for a positive c', still x^(1/2-o(1)). Modern zero-free-region
improvements alter the subexponential factor but do not remove this
power-size gap. This observation compares forms of bounds; no current
record numerical constant is asserted here.

The first unresolved lemma is therefore a one-sided cancellation
estimate such as (T), or the still weaker one-sided subpower growth
statement. Available absolute PNT error bounds do not establish it.
The finite-range checker can test J directly using (B), but no amount
of finite testing alone supplies the eventual assertion.

Next concrete test: transfer the existing finite g certification to J.
For 1<x<=2 the prime sum vanishes, so
J(x)=-4 sqrt(x)+log(x)+4<0 directly. For x>=2, g(log x)<=0 gives
J(x)<=-(C-1)log(x)+K<0: coarse rational bounds C-1>3/2, K<1/2,
and log(2)>1/3 suffice. Thus a verified g<=0 range through x=100
already proves J<0 on 1<x<=100; no separate stationary-point
computation is needed. Document that finite range separately from
the unsolved eventual assertion (T).

Sources: Nakamura--Suzuki (2023), equations (1.5), (1.7), (2.2), and
the standard Landau theorem for Laplace transforms of nonnegative
functions used in their converse proof. No novelty claim is made.
